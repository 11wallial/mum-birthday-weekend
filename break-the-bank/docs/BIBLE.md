# The House — a two-page bible

What the fiction is, so every writer, artist and cue points the same way.
This is a bible, not a novel: two pages, and anything that would make it
three belongs in a memo the CRT prints.

## Who you are

A debtor who has put themself up as the surety. Not a hero, not a thief —
someone who owed the House money before the first spin, could not cover it,
and signed the one instrument the House still accepts: their own life,
against the account, with the payout on the other side of it being enough
to walk out into. You were shown to a machine in the basement. You have no
name; the House has your account number. Everything you learn to do — hold
a reel, work the market, raise the stake, sign for a floor, bolt on a drum
— the House taught you, floor by floor, because a debtor who cannot play
cannot pay.

You are competent, and getting better. That is the arc. The machine does not
change; what you can do to it does. The late floors want foresight you do
not have on your first visit, and they are meant to: an early run ends
badly, and the run that clears the House is the one that learnt, floors
ago, which decisions were load-bearing.

## The frame

The basement is not a room. It is the House's simulation, and you are
playing it from inside a headset the House put on you — which is why the
machine can be rebuilt between floors, why the floors are seven versions of
the same basement, and why the House's people can be on every one of them.
The frame is not a cinematic; it has a job every spin. The surety is what
the simulation runs on, and as the House's hold on you tightens the render
itself fails — the picture tears, the colour comes apart, the grain comes
up, the brightness stutters. A covered floor is a clean print. A floor you
cannot reach is barely a picture at all.

The machine carries the same number as an instrument: the **surety
column** on its right flank, a glass sight-gauge with a red level, plumbed
into how much of you the House holds right now. Zero while the purse covers
what the close will charge; full when the spins left cannot reach it. It
moves on every spin, up on a dead one and down on a paying one, so a loss
is something that happens to you rather than an absence. The Clerk names it
once, at the first ante; after that the column does the talking.

The horror is bureaucratic, not supernatural. Nobody bleeds. The surety is
a clause, the column is a gauge, and the worst thing in the building is a
receipt.

## What the House is

A casino, in the sense that a bank is a building. It is seven floors of a
tower that was a mill, then a counting-house, then this, and it kept the
machinery from all three: steam, brass, the works. It does not cheat. It does
not need to. The terms are printed, the count is kept, the ante rises, and
the vig is charged before the ante because the House has never once let a
debt wait. It does *respond*: win loudly enough in one spin and the House
notices, says so, and sends one more of its people to the next floor. The
player should always be able to point at the spin and say the House did
that because I was winning — and the House would agree.

The House is never a person. It has staff — the croupier who marks a card,
the pit boss who comes over, whoever it is that has a quiet word — but they
are the House's hands, and the House speaks through paper: the contract,
the ledger on the CRT, the slate. It is polite. It is precise. It has all
the time in the world, and you have this floor's spins.

**The voice is contractual.** Dry, exact, faintly predatory, never cruel.
It counts things. It does not threaten; it informs. *Miss a payment and the
shortfall returns as principal with a penalty on top* is a sentence the House
would say; *you'll regret it* is not. Every string in the game — floor
intros, contract clauses, artifact flavour, death epitaphs, the memos — is
in this register. The commit history is accidentally already in it. Keep it.

## What the machine is

Yours, in the way a rented room is yours. Every artifact bolts onto it and
stays; by floor seven it is a machine nobody else would recognise, and that
is the point: the build is the character. It is volatile — steam, arc-light,
a lever you haul — because the House's machinery was never made safe, only
made to work. Every part does something. If a part cannot explain itself it
comes off.

## The seven floors, as a building

Basement, Casino, High Roller Room, Vault, Back Office, Engine Room, the
House. Each floor is a department of the same firm, and each hands you one
more thing the firm does: the basement teaches the machine; the casino sells
you parts; the high roller room lets you wager; the vault takes deposits; the
back office writes contracts; the engine room wires every machine on the
floor to yours; and the House, at the top, keeps the count. The count is the
only floor that plays against you. That is the reveal: the House was
watching the whole time, and floor seven is where it stops pretending
otherwise.

## The ending, and the offer

Clear the seventh ante and repay what you owe, and you have beaten the House.
The ledger prints a zero. The lights come up. That is the win, and it is
recorded as one.

Then the House makes its counter-offer. Your debt back, and a chair. Stay,
and the floors go on after hours — each dearer than the last, the shop still
open, the office still signing — until an ante is missed, and then the House
keeps you. This is the endless mode, and it is framed as exactly what it is:
a deal with the firm you just beat, on the firm's terms. The player who
walks away has won. The player who stays has decided that winning was not
the point, and the House has known that about them since the basement.

The tone of the ending recolours the whole run. Neither ending is a joke.

## Words the game uses

| Word | What it means here |
| --- | --- |
| **the ante** | What a floor costs to leave. Due when the spins run out. |
| **the vig** | Interest on the debt, in cash, before the ante. Never touches the principal. |
| **the count** | The House's attention. Rises with what you win, falls with what you don't. |
| **the skim / the cold deck / the pit boss** | The count's three answers, in order. |
| **the works** | Reels and rows bolted on for good. |
| **the back office** | Where contracts are signed. Nobody goes up without one. |
| **the slate** | Buying on the debt. A markup, and the House remembers. |
| **the market** | Reroll, sell back, the slate. Floor two's gift. |
| **after hours** | The floors past the seventh, for a run that stayed. |
| **the ledger** | The CRT on the machine. The House's side of the conversation. |
| **chips** | The House's scrip. Buys the draft, settles nothing. Paid for floors, for spins not taken, and by the bank symbol. |
| **settling early** | Leaving a floor with spins on the clock, once the ante is covered, for chips. |
| **the door** | The title, and the pause. Where the machine and the audit are chosen. |
| **the Clerk** | The voice on the tannoy that walks a new debtor through the basement. Not the House; the House's hands. |
| **the press** | Where the reel is edited: a symbol struck, printed or gilded, for chips. |
| **the machines** | What a run starts on. The House keeps seven; each opens through play. |

## Rules for anyone writing

1. The House informs. It does not threaten, mock, or console.
2. Numbers are always exact and always the numbers the simulation will
   charge. A prompt that quotes a price nobody is charged is a bug, not
   flavour.
3. The player is never named and never described. The House knows the
   account, not the person.
4. Nothing is a joke, and everything is slightly funny, because it is all so
   reasonable.
5. Menace scales with the count and the debt, never with the floor index.
   The memo on the CRT gets more familiar as the House gets more interested.
